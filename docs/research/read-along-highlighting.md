# Transcript Read-Along highlighting: evidence and design options

Research date: 2026-09-07. Scope: synchronized transcript highlighting during audiobook listening, prioritizing understanding and reading efficiency, then visual comfort. This is a review and proposed experiment; no app behavior changed.

## Conclusion

The studies located do **not establish a universal winner between one word and several words** for fluent adult audiobook readers. The most directly relevant controlled comparison found no statistically significant comprehension difference between word, sentence, and no highlighting, in adults with aphasia. Preference and measured performance can also diverge. Treat the highlight primarily as a location cue, preserve readable surrounding text, and evaluate alternatives with this app's audience.

A restrained current-word highlight with a short outgoing fade is a reasonable first design experiment. There is no direct evidence in the studies located that a fade improves comprehension, nor a validated fade duration. Phrase or sentence highlighting is worth comparing as a calmer alternative, but should not be described as scientifically superior.

## Primary research

| Study | Comparison and findings | Applicability and limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Brown et al., 2021, *Effect of Digital Highlighting on Reading Comprehension Given Text-to-Speech Technology for People with Aphasia*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7959096/) | 25 adults with chronic aphasia read/listened to newspaper passages with word, sentence, or no synchronized highlighting. Mean comprehension accuracy: 68.07%, 67.02%, and 65.5%; difference was not significant, p=.274. Most preferred: sentence 13, word 9, none 3. | Direct comparison of highlight granularity, but a clinical population, TTS at 145 words/minute, and comprehension rather than independent reading speed. Non-significance does not prove equivalence. No phrase condition or fades. |
| [Gerbier, Bailly & Bosse, 2018, *Audio–visual synchronization in reading while listening to texts*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0885230816300596) | 40 French children, grades 3–5, read/listened to short stories. Compared ordinary black text against grey text whose current words became black. Synchronized presentation produced fewer but longer fixations and fewer regressions; no orthographic-learning benefit and worse short-term learning of novel words' semantic categories. Most preferred the unsynchronized condition; poorer readers tended toward synchronization. | Direct audio-synchronized comparison, but children and incidental invented-word learning, not adult narrative comprehension. The contrast of surrounding text also changed, so results cannot isolate every aspect of highlighting. More orderly eye movements did not guarantee better learning. |
| [Rummens & Beier, online 2025; journal issue 2026, *Gaze-based word highlighting boosts reading performance*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40504601/) | 70 Danish second graders read aloud with and without words turning blue when fixated. Highlighting improved reading efficiency without reducing pronunciation accuracy or comprehension. Children nevertheless preferred static black text. | The highlight followed the reader's gaze, **not narration**. Beginner readers, not fluent adult audiobook listeners. Useful evidence that preference and performance should be measured separately; not evidence that narration-paced one-word highlighting is optimal. Abstract reviewed. |
| [Galimberti, Mora & Gilabert, 2023, *Audio-synchronized textual enhancement in foreign language pronunciation learning from videos*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2023.103078) | 58 university learners of English viewed videos with selected target words highlighted at different times. Synchronized enhancement increased audiovisual alignment and supported gains in word-recognition response times; unsynchronized enhancement also supported learning. | Adult evidence, but L2 pronunciation and selected vocabulary in captions. Does not compare continuous word versus phrase highlighting or establish a best mode for comprehension of an audiobook. |
| [Jung & Zhang, *Text-audio synchronized textual enhancement of collocations in reading-while-listening*, conference abstract](https://research.cuhk.edu.hk/en/publications/text-audio-synchronized-textual-enhancement-of-collocations-in-re-2/) | 87 Cantonese speakers read English stories in reading-only, read/listen, static-colouring, or synchronized-colouring conditions. Highlighting targeted adjective–pseudonoun pairs. Both colouring methods aided receptive collocation learning; their effectiveness was comparable. | Supports investigating meaningful multiword targets for language learning. Conference abstract only, not a full methods/results audit; no one-word comparator. It cannot establish that continuously highlighting phrases is best for general reading. The repository's publication/event dates disagree. |

No direct randomized comparison of outgoing fades, rounded versus square word backgrounds, or a fixed optimum number of simultaneously highlighted words was located. Studies of static keyword marking, phrase-based page layout, or speed-reading displays answer different questions and should not be substituted for such evidence.

## What the implementation currently suggests

The code review found an animated segment backdrop, but instantaneous word styling. `read-along-segment-item.tsx` gives the segment a rounded backdrop (radius 12) and 220 ms fade; the current word is a nested `Text` background without an outgoing-word lifecycle. `src/read-along/read-along-rendering.ts` uses accent colour at 0.32 alpha for the default highlight. The bold option changes weight to 600 and already documents reflow risk; colour and no-word-highlight options also exist.

`use-read-along-position.ts` polls at 150 ms. That can quantize transitions or miss sufficiently short words, particularly at faster playback rates. `src/read-along/read-along-sync.ts` clears the active word in timestamp gaps, which could add visible blinking. These are code-based hypotheses: no runtime profiling established their contribution to the reported jerkiness.

## First experiment

1. Verify timing first: record word transitions at normal and fast playback, including short words and timestamp gaps. Animation cannot repair skipped or late targets.
2. Keep text colour, weight, positions, and surrounding readability steady. Try an understated rounded backdrop for the current word over the existing subtle segment cue.
3. Bring in the new cue promptly; trial approximately 40–60 ms onset and 80–120 ms outgoing fade. Shorten at faster rates and retain at most one outgoing word. These are tuning starting points, not research findings. Fade the backdrop, not the word's legibility.
4. Cross-fade in place rather than sliding a shape across words or line breaks. Actual rounded inline backgrounds need measured word rectangles or suitable native text rendering; wrapping words into independent flex boxes risks changing text layout.
5. Compare the current word treatment, softened word highlighting, and the existing segment-only mode (Word highlight: None). Test a phrase-only alternative separately. A genuine phrase mode requires meaningful phrase boundaries; an arbitrary moving three-word window is a separate experiment. A fully unhighlighted baseline would also require disabling the segment cue, which the current None option does not do.

Use matched passages with counterbalanced order and the same narration rate. Prioritize comprehension questions, including inference, then loss-of-place/rewind frequency and sustained comfort. To investigate usable speed, compare the highest rate at which comprehension remains acceptable rather than claiming animation alone makes reading faster. Collect preference separately, and include users who find tracking difficult as well as fluent readers. A small pilot can guide product decisions but cannot establish population-wide superiority.
